CBC Curriculum Alignment: Volumes of Solids falls under the Measurement and Mensuration strand of the Applied Mathematics component in the Kenyan Senior Secondary curriculum, as approved by the Kenya Institute of Curriculum Development (KICD). It is examined in KCSE Paper 1 and underpins real-world applications in engineering, architecture, and physical sciences. This lesson progresses from foundational 3D shapes in Junior Secondary, through composite and similar solids in Senior Secondary, to volumes of revolution at university level — covering all competency levels in the Basic Education Curriculum Framework (BECF) and aligned to TSC teaching standards and MoE assessment guidelines.

Section 01 · Introduction

Why Volume Matters

Junior Secondary

Volume measures the amount of three-dimensional space enclosed within a solid object. It is one of the most practically important mathematical concepts — from estimating how much water fills a tank, to calculating how much concrete is needed for a building foundation.

Volume in Everyday Life in Kenya

Volume is not an abstract concept — it governs decisions made every day. A water vendor needs to know the volume of their storage tank to price deliveries correctly. A farmer constructing a grain silo must calculate the cylindrical capacity to avoid over-ordering materials. An architect designing a school building must compute the volume of concrete required for each column. A nurse calculating a medicine dose in a syringe uses volume. Every career in the STEM pathway — engineering, medicine, agriculture, architecture — depends on volumetric reasoning.

The KICD CBC curriculum introduces volumes in Junior Secondary as part of the Measurement strand, building on area concepts. In Senior Secondary, the topic deepens to include composite solids, scale factors for similar solids, and real-world design problems. At university level, calculus-based methods allow volumes of curved shapes that cannot be computed from simple formulas.

Units of Volume

Volume is measured in cubic units. The choice of unit depends on the size of the object being measured. For small objects (laboratory containers, household items), we use cubic centimetres (cm³) or millilitres (1 mL = 1 cm³). For room-sized objects, cubic metres (m³) apply. For large-scale engineering (dams, reservoirs), megalitres or cubic kilometres may be used. Always convert all linear dimensions to the same unit before computing volume.

Unit Conversions for Volume
1 cm³
= 1 mL (millilitre)
1 litre
= 1 000 cm³ = 1 000 mL
1 m³
= 1 000 000 cm³ = 1 000 litres
1 km³
= 10⁹ m³
📝
KCSE Exam Note: Volume problems in KCSE Paper 1 often combine unit conversion with the volume formula. A very common error is mixing cm and m in the same calculation. Always convert all given lengths to the same unit at the very start, before applying any formula.

Section 02 · Basic Solids

Prisms and Cuboids

Junior Secondary

A prism is any solid with two identical, parallel polygonal faces (the bases) joined by rectangular side faces. The volume of any prism is found by the same universal rule: multiply the base area by the perpendicular height.

Volume Formulas — Prisms
General prism
V = A_base × h
Cube (side a)
V = a³
Cuboid (rectangular prism)
V = l × w × h
Triangular prism
V = (½ × base × height of triangle) × length of prism
Trapezoidal prism
V = ½(a + b) × h_trap × l    (a, b parallel sides; h_trap trapezium height)

Understanding the Prism Formula

The formula V = A_base × h works because you are essentially stacking infinitely many identical thin cross-sections on top of each other. This is the same principle used in calculus — Cavalieri's Principle states that if two solids have the same height and equal cross-sectional areas at every level, they have equal volumes. This is why a slanted prism has the same volume as an upright prism with the same base and perpendicular height.

Worked Examples — Prisms and Cuboids
Example 1 — Cube A storage cube has side length 15 cm. Find its volume in litres.
Step 1 Apply V = a³ = 15³ = 15 × 15 × 15 = 3 375 cm³.
Step 2 Convert to litres: 3 375 ÷ 1 000 = 3.375 litres.
V = 3 375 cm³ = 3.375 litres
Example 2 — Cuboid (word problem) A water storage tank is 2.5 m long, 1.8 m wide, and 1.2 m deep. How many litres of water does it hold when full?
Step 1 All dimensions are in metres. V = l × w × h = 2.5 × 1.8 × 1.2.
Step 2 V = 2.5 × 1.8 = 4.5. Then 4.5 × 1.2 = 5.4 m³.
Step 3 Convert: 1 m³ = 1 000 litres. So 5.4 m³ = 5 400 litres.
Capacity = 5.4 m³ = 5 400 litres
Example 3 — Triangular Prism A triangular prism has a right-angled triangular base with legs 6 cm and 8 cm. The prism is 20 cm long. Find its volume.
Step 1 Area of triangular base = ½ × 6 × 8 = 24 cm².
Step 2 V = A_base × length = 24 × 20 = 480 cm³.
V = 480 cm³
Example 4 — Finding a missing dimension A cuboid has volume 360 cm³. Its length is 12 cm and width is 5 cm. Find its height.
Step 1 Use V = l × w × h. Substitute: 360 = 12 × 5 × h = 60h.
Step 2 h = 360 / 60 = 6 cm.
Height = 6 cm

Section 03 · Circular Solids

Cylinders

Junior Secondary

A cylinder is a prism whose base is a circle. It is one of the most common shapes in engineering and everyday life — water pipes, cooking pots, oil drums, pillars, and gas cylinders are all essentially cylindrical.

Cylinder Formulas
Volume
V = πr²h
Curved surface area
CSA = 2πrh
Total surface area
TSA = 2πrh + 2πr² = 2πr(h + r)
Hollow cylinder (pipe)
V = π(R² − r²)h    (R = outer radius, r = inner radius)
Worked Examples — Cylinders
Example 1 — Standard cylinder A cylindrical tank has radius 5 cm and height 12 cm. Find its volume, giving the answer in terms of π and as a decimal (use π ≈ 3.142).
Step 1 V = πr²h = π × 5² × 12 = π × 25 × 12 = 300π cm³.
Step 2 Decimal: 300 × 3.142 = 942.6 cm³ (to 1 d.p.).
V = 300π cm³ ≈ 942.6 cm³
Example 2 — KCSE-style: find radius A cylinder holds 2 litres of water when full. Its height is 25 cm. Find its radius, correct to 1 decimal place. (Use π = 3.142)
Step 1 Convert: 2 litres = 2 000 cm³. So V = 2 000 cm³ and h = 25 cm.
Step 2 From V = πr²h: r² = V / (πh) = 2 000 / (3.142 × 25) = 2 000 / 78.55 ≈ 25.463.
Step 3 r = √25.463 ≈ 5.0 cm.
Radius ≈ 5.0 cm
Example 3 — Hollow pipe A metal pipe has outer diameter 10 cm, inner diameter 8 cm, and length 50 cm. Find the volume of metal used.
Step 1 Outer radius R = 5 cm, inner radius r = 4 cm, h = 50 cm.
Step 2 V = π(R² − r²)h = π(25 − 16) × 50 = π × 9 × 50 = 450π cm³.
Step 3 Decimal: 450 × 3.142 ≈ 1 413.9 cm³.
Volume of metal = 450π ≈ 1 413.9 cm³

Section 04 · Tapering Solids

Pyramids and Cones

Senior Secondary

Pyramids and cones are "pointed" solids where the cross-section shrinks to zero at the apex. Because of this tapering, their volumes are exactly one-third of the corresponding prism or cylinder with the same base and height — a result that can be proved by calculus or by Cavalieri's Principle.

Pyramids and Cones — Formulas
General pyramid
V = ⅓ × A_base × h
Square pyramid (side a, height h)
V = ⅓ a²h
Rectangular pyramid
V = ⅓ × l × w × h
Cone
V = ⅓πr²h
Slant height of cone
l = √(r² + h²)    (by Pythagoras)
Frustum (truncated cone)
V = ⅓πh(R² + Rr + r²)    (R = large radius, r = small radius)

Why the Factor of ⅓?

The factor ⅓ arises because the cross-sectional area of a cone or pyramid shrinks quadratically as you move from base to apex. If you integrate the area at each height from 0 to h, you get exactly one-third of the base area multiplied by h. This is one of the earliest non-trivial results in mensuration, known to ancient Egyptian mathematicians over 3 500 years ago. You can also verify it physically: a cone-shaped vessel filled three times and poured into a cylinder of identical base and height will fill the cylinder exactly.

Worked Examples — Pyramids and Cones
Example 1 — Square pyramid The Great Pyramid of Giza has a square base of side 230 m and a height of approximately 138 m. Estimate its volume.
Step 1 A_base = 230² = 52 900 m².
Step 2 V = ⅓ × 52 900 × 138 = ⅓ × 7 300 200 = 2 433 400 m³ (approximately 2.43 million cubic metres).
V ≈ 2 433 400 m³ ≈ 2.43 × 10⁶ m³
Example 2 — Cone (tent problem) A conical tent has base radius 7 m and height 9 m. Find: (a) the volume, (b) the slant height.
Part a V = ⅓πr²h = ⅓ × π × 49 × 9 = ⅓ × 441π = 147π ≈ 147 × 3.142 ≈ 461.9 m³.
Part b Slant height l = √(r² + h²) = √(49 + 81) = √130 ≈ 11.4 m.
(a) V = 147π ≈ 461.9 m³    (b) l ≈ 11.4 m
Example 3 — Frustum A bucket (frustum shape) has top radius 20 cm, bottom radius 12 cm, and height 30 cm. Find its volume.
Step 1 Identify: R = 20, r = 12, h = 30.
Step 2 V = ⅓πh(R² + Rr + r²) = ⅓ × π × 30 × (400 + 240 + 144).
Step 3 = 10π × 784 = 7 840π ≈ 7 840 × 3.142 ≈ 24 633 cm³ ≈ 24.6 litres.
V = 7 840π ≈ 24 633 cm³ ≈ 24.6 litres
Example 4 — Find height given volume A cone has volume 462 cm³ and base radius 7 cm. Find its perpendicular height. (Use π = 22/7)
Step 1 From V = ⅓πr²h: h = 3V / (πr²) = 3 × 462 / (22/7 × 49).
Step 2 = 1 386 / (22 × 7) = 1 386 / 154 = 9 cm.
Height = 9 cm

Section 05 · Curved Solids

Spheres and Hemispheres

Senior Secondary

A sphere is perfectly symmetric in all directions from its centre — every point on its surface is exactly the same distance (the radius) from the centre. Spherical shapes appear in ball bearings, planets, water droplets, and storage tanks. The volume formula requires a factor of 4/3π — a result most elegantly derived by calculus.

Sphere and Hemisphere Formulas
Volume of sphere
V = (4/3)πr³
Surface area of sphere
SA = 4πr²
Volume of hemisphere
V = (2/3)πr³
Curved SA of hemisphere
CSA = 2πr²
Total SA of hemisphere
TSA = 2πr² + πr² = 3πr²
Given volume, find r
r = ∛(3V / 4π)
Worked Examples — Spheres and Hemispheres
Example 1 — Sphere volume A solid metal sphere has radius 6 cm. Find its volume in terms of π, and as a decimal to 1 d.p.
Step 1 V = (4/3)πr³ = (4/3) × π × 6³ = (4/3) × π × 216 = 4 × 72π = 288π cm³.
Step 2 Decimal: 288 × 3.142 = 904.9 cm³.
V = 288π cm³ ≈ 904.9 cm³
Example 2 — Find radius from volume A spherical balloon has volume 904.32 cm³. Find its radius (use π = 3.142).
Step 1 From V = (4/3)πr³: r³ = 3V / (4π) = 3 × 904.32 / (4 × 3.142) = 2 712.96 / 12.568 ≈ 215.85.
Step 2 r = ∛215.85 ≈ 6.0 cm.
Radius = 6.0 cm
Example 3 — Hemisphere word problem A hemispherical bowl has an internal radius of 9 cm. Find how many full cups of water (each cup holds 200 cm³) it can hold.
Step 1 V_hemisphere = (2/3)πr³ = (2/3) × π × 729 = 486π ≈ 486 × 3.142 ≈ 1 527.0 cm³.
Step 2 Number of cups = 1 527 ÷ 200 = 7.635 → 7 full cups (take the whole number part only).
The bowl holds 7 full cups of water.

Section 06 · Combined Shapes

Composite Solids

Senior Secondary

Most real-world objects are not perfect simple solids — they combine multiple 3D shapes. A composite solid is one formed by joining (adding) or carving out (subtracting) two or more basic solids. The strategy is always the same: identify the component shapes, find each volume separately, then add or subtract.

Addition Strategy
Used when shapes are joined together. Example: a cylinder topped with a hemisphere (like a hot water cylinder). V_total = V_cylinder + V_hemisphere.
Subtraction Strategy
Used when a shape is hollowed out or has a cavity. Example: a solid cylinder with a conical hole bored into one end. V_solid = V_cylinder − V_cone.
Mixed Strategy
Some solids require both. Always sketch the shape first, identify every component, and write the formula before substituting numbers.
Worked Examples — Composite Solids
Example 1 — Cylinder + Hemisphere (Addition) A hot water tank consists of a cylinder of radius 40 cm and height 100 cm, with a hemispherical cap of the same radius on each end. Find the total volume.
Step 1 V_cylinder = πr²h = π × 1 600 × 100 = 160 000π cm³.
Step 2 Two hemispheres = one full sphere: V_sphere = (4/3)πr³ = (4/3) × π × 64 000 = 256 000π/3 cm³.
Step 3 V_total = 160 000π + 256 000π/3 = (480 000π + 256 000π)/3 = 736 000π/3 ≈ 770 634 cm³ ≈ 770.6 litres.
Total volume ≈ 770 634 cm³ ≈ 770.6 litres
Example 2 — Cylinder with conical hole (Subtraction) A solid cylinder of radius 6 cm and height 20 cm has a conical hole of the same radius and height 8 cm bored into one end. Find the remaining volume. (Use π = 3.142)
Step 1 V_cylinder = π × 36 × 20 = 720π cm³.
Step 2 V_cone = ⅓ × π × 36 × 8 = 96π cm³.
Step 3 Remaining volume = 720π − 96π = 624π ≈ 624 × 3.142 ≈ 1 960.6 cm³.
Remaining volume = 624π ≈ 1 960.6 cm³
Example 3 — Real-world: grain silo A grain silo consists of a cylinder of diameter 4 m and height 8 m, topped by a conical roof of the same base radius and slant height 3 m. Find the total volume that can be stored inside. (Use π = 3.142)
Step 1 r = 2 m for both shapes. For the cone: l = 3, r = 2, so h_cone = √(l² − r²) = √(9 − 4) = √5 ≈ 2.236 m.
Step 2 V_cylinder = π × 4 × 8 = 32π ≈ 100.54 m³.
Step 3 V_cone = ⅓ × π × 4 × 2.236 = (8.944/3)π ≈ 2.981π ≈ 9.37 m³.
Step 4 Total ≈ 100.54 + 9.37 = 109.91 m³ ≈ 109 900 litres of grain.
Total silo capacity ≈ 109.9 m³ ≈ 109 900 litres

Section 07 · Scale Factors

Similar Solids and Scale Factors

Senior Secondary

Two solids are similar if they have exactly the same shape but different sizes — every corresponding length is in the same ratio. Understanding how volume scales with the linear scale factor is essential for solving proportion-based KCSE problems and real-world manufacturing challenges.

Similar Solids — Scale Factor Relationships
Linear scale factor
k = L₂ / L₁    (ratio of any corresponding lengths)
Area scale factor
A₂ / A₁ = k²
Volume scale factor
V₂ / V₁ = k³
Finding k from volumes
k = ∛(V₂ / V₁)
Finding k from areas
k = √(A₂ / A₁)

Why Volume Scales as k³

When every linear dimension is multiplied by k, area (which involves two dimensions multiplied together) scales as k × k = k². Volume involves three dimensions, so it scales as k × k × k = k³. This has profound practical consequences: if you double the size of a container (k = 2), its volume becomes 2³ = 8 times larger, not 2 times larger. A water tank with twice the radius and height holds 8 times as much water — something that surprises people who think linearly.

Worked Examples — Similar Solids
Example 1 — Classic KCSE problem Two similar cones have base radii 3 cm and 6 cm. The smaller cone has volume 24 cm³. Find the volume of the larger cone.
Step 1 Linear scale factor: k = 6/3 = 2.
Step 2 Volume scale factor: k³ = 2³ = 8.
Step 3 V_large = 8 × V_small = 8 × 24 = 192 cm³.
Volume of larger cone = 192 cm³
Example 2 — Find the linear scale factor Two similar spheres have volumes 27 cm³ and 125 cm³. Find: (a) the ratio of their radii, (b) the ratio of their surface areas.
Part a Volume ratio = 125/27. k³ = 125/27 → k = ∛(125/27) = 5/3. Radii ratio = 5:3.
Part b Area ratio = k² = (5/3)² = 25/9. Surface areas are in ratio 25:9.
(a) Radii ratio = 5:3    (b) Surface areas ratio = 25:9
Example 3 — KCSE-style multi-step A manufacturer makes two similar cylindrical tins. The smaller tin has diameter 8 cm and holds 600 cm³ of paint. The larger tin has diameter 12 cm. Find: (a) the volume of the larger tin, (b) the height of the larger tin.
Part a k = 12/8 = 3/2. Volume ratio = k³ = (3/2)³ = 27/8. V_large = 600 × 27/8 = 600 × 3.375 = 2 025 cm³.
Part b From V_small = πr²h: 600 = π × 16 × h → h_small = 600/(16π) ≈ 11.94 cm. h_large = k × h_small = (3/2) × 11.94 ≈ 17.9 cm.
(a) V_large = 2 025 cm³    (b) h_large ≈ 17.9 cm

Section 08 · University / A-Level

Volumes by Calculus (Volumes of Revolution)

University

When a curve is rotated about an axis, it sweeps out a three-dimensional solid. The volume of this solid of revolution can be computed exactly using integration. This technique extends mensuration far beyond the reach of simple formulas and is used in engineering to design turbine blades, lenses, and precision components.

Methods for Volumes of Revolution
Disc method (about x-axis)
V = π ∫ₐᵇ [f(x)]² dx
Disc method (about y-axis)
V = π ∫_c^d [g(y)]² dy    (where x = g(y))
Washer method (hollow, about x-axis)
V = π ∫ₐᵇ ([f(x)]² − [g(x)]²) dx
Shell method (about y-axis)
V = 2π ∫ₐᵇ x · f(x) dx
When to use disc/washer
Rotation about x-axis, integrating in x. Cross-sections are circles (discs) or annuli (washers).
When to use shell
Rotation about y-axis but integrating in x — often avoids the need to re-express x in terms of y.

The Disc Method — Intuition

Imagine slicing the solid of revolution into infinitely many thin discs perpendicular to the axis of rotation. Each disc at position x has radius f(x) and thickness dx, giving a volume of π[f(x)]²dx. Summing all these infinitely thin discs from x = a to x = b via integration gives the total volume. This is the continuous generalisation of the formula V = A × h for a prism.

Worked Examples — Volumes of Revolution
Example 1 — Derive the sphere formula By rotating y = √(r² − x²) (the upper semicircle) about the x-axis from x = −r to x = r, derive V = (4/3)πr³.
Step 1 Use disc method: V = π ∫₋ᵣʳ [f(x)]² dx = π ∫₋ᵣʳ (r² − x²) dx.
Step 2 Integrate: π [r²x − x³/3]₋ᵣʳ.
Step 3 At x = r: r³ − r³/3 = 2r³/3. At x = −r: −r³ + r³/3 = −2r³/3. Subtract: 2r³/3 − (−2r³/3) = 4r³/3.
Step 4 V = π × 4r³/3 = (4/3)πr³ ✓ — the standard sphere formula is derived.
V = (4/3)πr³ ✓ (proved by integration)
Example 2 — Disc method Find the volume of the solid formed when the region bounded by y = x², x = 0, x = 3, and y = 0 is rotated 360° about the x-axis.
Step 1 V = π ∫₀³ (x²)² dx = π ∫₀³ x⁴ dx.
Step 2 = π [x⁵/5]₀³ = π (243/5 − 0) = 243π/5.
Step 3 ≈ 243 × 3.142 / 5 ≈ 152.8 cubic units.
V = 243π/5 ≈ 152.8 cubic units
Example 3 — Washer method The region between y = √x and y = x (for 0 ≤ x ≤ 1) is rotated about the x-axis. Find the volume of the solid formed.
Step 1 Outer function: f(x) = √x (larger). Inner function: g(x) = x (smaller). Check: at x = 0.25, √0.25 = 0.5 > 0.25 ✔.
Step 2 V = π ∫₀¹ [(√x)² − x²] dx = π ∫₀¹ [x − x²] dx.
Step 3 = π [x²/2 − x³/3]₀¹ = π (1/2 − 1/3) = π × 1/6 = π/6 cubic units.
V = π/6 ≈ 0.524 cubic units
Example 4 — Shell method Find the volume of the solid formed when the region bounded by y = 2x − x² and y = 0 is rotated about the y-axis. Use the shell method.
Step 1 The curve crosses the x-axis at y = 0: 2x − x² = 0 → x(2 − x) = 0 → x = 0 and x = 2. Limits: a = 0, b = 2.
Step 2 Shell method: V = 2π ∫₀² x(2x − x²) dx = 2π ∫₀² (2x² − x³) dx.
Step 3 = 2π [2x³/3 − x⁴/4]₀² = 2π [(16/3) − (16/4)] = 2π [16/3 − 4] = 2π × 4/3 = 8π/3.
V = 8π/3 ≈ 8.38 cubic units
📝
Pappus's Centroid Theorem provides an elegant shortcut for volumes of revolution: the volume of a solid of revolution equals the area of the 2D region multiplied by the distance travelled by the centroid of that region. For a region of area A whose centroid is at distance d̄ from the axis of rotation: V = 2π d̄ A. This is particularly useful for standard shapes like rectangles and circles where the centroid position is immediately known.

Common Errors in Volume Problems

✗ WrongV of cone = πr²h (same as cylinder)
✓ RightV of cone = ⅓πr²h (one-third of cylinder)
The ⅓ factor is the most commonly dropped term in cone and pyramid problems. Always include it.
✗ WrongSimilar cones, k = 2: V_large = 2 × V_small
✓ RightV_large = k³ × V_small = 8 × V_small
Volume scales as the CUBE of the linear scale factor, not as k itself. This is the most common error in similar solids problems.
✗ WrongMixing units: r = 5 cm, h = 1.2 m → V = π(5²)(1.2)
✓ RightConvert first: h = 120 cm → V = π(25)(120) = 3 000π cm³
Always convert every dimension to the same unit before substituting into any formula. A common exam trap.
✗ WrongUsing diameter instead of radius: V = π(10²)h
✓ RightIf diameter = 10, then r = 5: V = π(5²)h = 25πh
All volume formulas use the RADIUS. When a problem gives diameter, halve it immediately before substituting.
✗ WrongV of revolution: V = ∫f(x)dx (missing π and the square)
✓ RightV = π∫[f(x)]²dx (must square the function and multiply by π)
In the disc method, you are computing the area of circles (πr²). The function gives the radius r = f(x), so it must be squared. Forgetting either π or the square is common.
✗ WrongFrustum volume: V = ⅓πh(R + r)²
✓ RightV = ⅓πh(R² + Rr + r²) — three terms, not (R+r)²
(R + r)² = R² + 2Rr + r² which has coefficient 2 in the middle term. The correct frustum formula has coefficient 1. Memorise it carefully.
